\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% [P1] Entonnoir haut : swarm robotics -> intralogistique.
% [P2] Entonnoir bas : MAPF -> PIBT (related work concis).
Swarm robotics trades a few heavily instrumented robots for many small, cheap units
that coordinate in a decentralized way, so that system throughput scales with the size
of the fleet rather than with per-unit capability. Intralogistics --- automated
warehouses and parcel-sorting floors of the Kiva type, where mobile robots shuttle
goods between storage and picking stations --- is its flagship application and the one
we target here~\cite{TODO:swarm_survey}\cite{TODO:warehouse}.% Wen, He & Zhu 2018 (SwarmRobotics2018Wen) ; Wurman, D'Andrea & Mountz 2008 (Coordinating2008Wurman, Kiva)

The coordination kernel of such a fleet is Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF): routing
$N$ agents from their start cells to $N$ distinct goal cells on a shared graph without
vertex or swap collisions~\cite{TODO:MAPF}.% Stern et al. 2019 (MAPF2019Stern)
Optimal MAPF is NP-hard~\cite{okumura22priority}, so deployed systems favour fast,
scalable sub-optimal solvers. PIBT (Priority Inheritance with Backtracking) is one
such solver~\cite{okumura22priority}: it plans a single synchronous step at a time,
guarantees that every agent eventually reaches its goal on biconnected graphs, and runs
in predictable, near-real-time computation --- properties that make it a natural fit
for on-board, lifelong operation rather than offline batch planning.

The DotBot is an open-source, low-cost wheeled micro-robot. It is localized by a single
Lighthouse\,v2 (LH2) base station and networked through MARI, a TSCH-over-BLE mesh
protocol, giving sparse external positioning and constrained, contention-prone
communication --- exactly the regime a real deployment must tolerate~\cite{alvarado24dotbot}.

% [P4] Contribution : validation à deux niveaux (L0 simu -> L1 robots réels), gap.
We validate PIBT for DotBot warehouse-style coordination at two levels on a shared
occupancy axis $\rho = N/\text{cells}$: \emph{L0}, an abstract-grid simulation that
fixes the algorithmic ceiling, and \emph{L1}, the same planner driving real DotBots.
Joining the two on $\rho$ quantifies the \textbf{plan-to-real gap}.
Our thesis is that the simulated ceiling does not survive contact with hardware: the
simulation predicts cheap, near-linear coordination up to an efficiency knee at
$\rho \approx 0.60$, yet real per-instance reliability already collapses at
$\rho \approx 0.30$, roughly half the simulated value.

\section{PIBT Algorithm}
\label{sec:pbit}
% Explique PIBT depuis Okumura 2022. Reste concis.
PIBT solves MAPF iteratively by prioritized planning in a unit-length time
window~\cite{okumura22priority}. At each timestep every agent carries a unique priority
and the agents commit to their next cell in decreasing priority order, each choosing the
neighbouring cell closest to its goal that no higher-priority agent has reserved. When a
high-priority agent is blocked by a lower-priority one, the blocker temporarily
\emph{inherits} the higher priority and is forced to give way; if it then finds no valid
move, it \emph{backtracks} and the caller tries its next candidate. On any graph where
every pair of adjacent nodes lies on a simple cycle --- an obstacle-free rectangular grid
is biconnected and qualifies --- this scheme guarantees \emph{reachability}: every agent
reaches its goal within finite time, although not necessarily all at the same timestep.

One planning step costs $\mathcal{O}\!\left(|A|\,(\Delta(G)+\log|A|)\right)$ and stays
within a few milliseconds even for hundreds of agents, so the per-step runtime is
predictable. Because every decision is a local interaction between neighbouring agents,
PIBT is readily decentralized and extends to lifelong pickup-and-delivery
(MAPD)~\cite{okumura22priority}, the formulation closest to a working warehouse.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% [Setup]
\textbf{Setup.}
The same PIBT planner drives both levels. The physical arena is $2000\times2000$\,mm
with a single LH2 anchor. L1 sweeps a $5\times5$ grid (25 cells, $400$\,mm) and adds one
$8\times8$ control (64 cells, $250$\,mm). DotBots are commanded one synchronous PIBT step
at a time through a barrier: the planner advances one step, the bots are sent to their
next cells, and the loop waits until every bot is within a $75$\,mm arrival threshold
(tightened from $100$\,mm) or an $8$\,s per-step timeout elapses, re-synchronising each
bot's cell from LH2 before the next step. Commands go through the PyDotBot
stack~\cite{TODO:pydotbot} over MARI/LH2~\cite{alvarado24dotbot}.% PyDotBot controller (Geovane / pydotbot package)
We place one DotBot per cell and draw starts and goals uniformly at random per seed. The
hardware logs hold \textbf{8 configurations} ($N=4$--$10$ on the $5\times5$ grid plus
$N=4$ on the $8\times8$ grid) over \textbf{67 runs} total.

% [Évaluation]
\textbf{Evaluation.}
For each run \texttt{l1\_metrics.py} logs makespan (steps), wall-time (s), step-timeouts
(bot\,$\times$\,step events that miss the $8$\,s barrier), overshoot (mean/max stop
distance to the target cell, mm) and throughput (goals\,/\,min). We separate two notions
of success: \emph{per-agent} $=$ arrived$/N$, and \emph{per-instance} $=$ all $N$ arrived.
Only the per-instance notion is comparable to L0 and to Okumura's reachability criterion,
so we report it as the headline metric.

% [Résultats L0 — court] plafond algorithmique = borne haute.
\textbf{L0 --- the algorithmic ceiling.}
On the $5\times5$ grid ($30$ seeds, $100$-step cap), per-agent success stays high across
the entire sweep, from $98.3\%$ at $\rho=0.08$ to $92.1\%$ at $\rho=1.00$: reachability
is guaranteed and only the conservative step cap clips it. The informative ceiling is
therefore in \emph{cost}, not success. Both sum-of-costs and mean makespan remain
near-linear up to $\rho=0.60$ ($N=15$: makespan $9.1$ steps, sum-of-costs $60.2$, detour
ratio $1.46$) and then accelerate sharply ($\rho=0.80$: makespan $14.6$, sum-of-costs
$101.7$; $\rho=1.00$: makespan $27.3$, sum-of-costs $250.7$). This $\rho\approx0.60$ knee
is the algorithmic upper bound --- the densest packing at which the planner still keeps
per-robot cost proportional to travel distance.

% [Résultats L1 — PLUS DÉTAILLÉ, c'est le cœur]
\textbf{L1 --- real DotBots.}
Per-instance success is $100\%$ for $N=4$--$7$ ($\rho\le0.28$): all $25$ runs delivered
every agent, with makespans of $5.2$--$7.8$ steps, $16$--$35$\,s wall-time, $69$--$90$\,mm
mean overshoot and $\le2$ step-timeouts per run. It then degrades monotonically with
density: $96.3\%$ at $N=8$ ($\rho=0.32$, $26/27$ runs), $80\%$ at $N=9$ ($\rho=0.36$,
$4/5$) and $71\%$ at $N=10$ ($\rho=0.40$, $5/7$). The degradation is physical, not
algorithmic, and three mechanisms compound. (i)~\emph{Footprint versus cell size}: a
DotBot needs room for a half-turn to reach the next waypoint; in tight cells it overshoots
the $75$\,mm threshold (mean overshoot stays $56$--$90$\,mm, with maxima above $600$\,mm),
the step barrier times out (timeouts climb from $\approx2$ to $5$ to $7$ per run over
$N=8\!\to\!10$, wall-time from $43$ to $90$\,s), and the next replan sends it back ---
trapping it in a two-cell oscillation. This is exactly the signature observed on a dense
$8\times8$ / $250$\,mm run, where only $3/6$ robots reached their goals. (ii)~\emph{Timeout
cascade}: because PIBT advances as one synchronous step, a single bot that misses the
barrier desynchronises its neighbours' reserved cells and breaks the local coherence PIBT
depends on, so one initial timeout can fail several agents at once. (iii)~\emph{Residual
rotations}: our planner forbids 2-agent swaps but not general $3{+}$-agent rotations (no
PIBT$'$), which on hardware surface as the very oscillations we observe. The net effect is
a real per-instance reliability ceiling at $\rho\approx0.30$ --- about half the simulated
cost knee at $\rho\approx0.60$ --- with throughput peaking near $24$ goals\,/\,min at
$N=6$ and falling to $\approx10$ goals\,/\,min once $N=10$ pushes the fleet past that
ceiling.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
DotBots driven by PIBT do perform warehouse-style multi-agent coordination, but the
densest occupancy that is \emph{reliable} on hardware is roughly half of what the
simulation's cost curve would permit ($\rho\approx0.30$ versus $\rho\approx0.60$). This is
a different question from Okumura et al.'s robot demonstration~\cite{okumura22priority},
which established \emph{feasibility} on the Toio platform using a position-encoded mat and
point-to-point BLE. We instead characterise \emph{reliability} under sparse external
localization (a single LH2 anchor) and constrained mesh communication
(MARI/TSCH)~\cite{alvarado24dotbot}, and we pinpoint where it breaks. Future work moves
from one-shot MAPF to lifelong Multi-Agent Pickup and Delivery (MAPD), the warehouse-native
formulation~\cite{TODO:MAPD}; runs MAPD in a decentralized fashion over the 6TiSCH mesh;% Flammini, Hawes & Lacerda 2025 (MAPD2025Flammini)
and validates at scale with our industrial partner, La Poste.
